What did groceries cost in the 1950s?

In 1953, a pound of white bread averaged 16.4¢, a dozen eggs 69.8¢, and half a gallon of delivered milk 46.8¢ in the federal retail-price series. Regular gasoline averaged 28.7¢ a gallon.

Those small prices came with different wages. The federal minimum was 75¢ an hour in 1953, for workers covered by it. Eggs therefore took about 56 minutes of work at that rate, before taxes. It is a useful comparison, not a complete account of affordability.

Historical Statistics of the United States, Part 1, p. 213, series E 187–202; EIA, Annual Energy Review, table 5.24: regular gasoline, nominal prices; Department of Labor, History of Federal Minimum Wage Rates.

The kitchen on film was also a sales pitch.

A Word to the Wives (1955) shows a kitchen in peach and turquoise, appliances, and a carefully staged household. It is color footage from the period, but it is also a promotional film: evidence of what companies wanted families to want.

We keep the original title and date with each frame. A scene from 1955 does not become a scene from someone’s birth year just because it appears in their booklet.

A Word to the Wives (1955), promotional film, Prelinger Archives.

A supermarket, and the world outside its frame.

Shopping Can Be Fun (1957) takes the camera to Hillsdale Shopping Center. Its meat counter and parking lot give us details that a black-and-white price table cannot: lettering on signs, clothing, colors, and the arrangement of the store.

A promotional camera gives us a selected view. It cannot stand for every American household, income, region, or experience. We use the records to check price claims and avoid turning an attractive scene into a claim that life was better for everybody.

Shopping Can Be Fun (1957), promotional film, Prelinger Archives.

What did families cook?

The USDA’s 1950 Family Fare includes meat loaf, upside-down cake, and brownies. Our free recipe cards reproduce those three recipes from pages 34, 81, and 85. They show what a government kitchen recommended, not a survey of what every family ate.
